## Day One, August 7

After a night sleeping near the trailhead, we hiked in with
good weather. The packs were heavy, but as I think about the
trip later, I'm amazed at how easy this made things for
many days to follow. A single base camp creates an almost
"European Alps" situation with short approaches and reasonable
days.

We found a camp about 10 minutes walk below Deep Lake after
5 hours of walking. We set things up, took a short rest, then
hiked up to do the Grade II "Railroad Tracks" route on
Haystack Mountain just above us. With a grade of 5.8, it should
be reasonable for a first day. It would also let us get
familiar with the descent, called the "Grassy Goat Trail."

I led the initial pitch, combining with the next for a 
full 90 meter lead to a tied-off bush. Robert got the
next pitch, with tricky but enjoyable moves. However,
there was a point with 2 cracks above a vertical
section. The crack on the left is protectable, but the
one on the right is full of grass. He ended up on the
right, and made sketchy face moves to go left. I was
following in the same place and I just asked Robert to
hold the rope so I could tension over to the left.
I grow cagey in my advanced age!

I led a short pitch above this, no special difficulties.
Then Robert led a crux pitch up to and beyond a
"prominent detached block." Really great climbing!
However, it was a lot longer than the guidebook indicated.
Rather than being 4 pitches, it was more like 7.

Anyway, I got us up to terrain where we could unrope and
bag the summit. Then we hiked down and intersected with the
Grassy Goat Trail at a cairn. The trail really begins more
from the north.

It was an interesting descent...very exposed, but reasonable.
With ordinary tennis shoes, I chose to rappel at the one
anchor we found while Robert downclimbed.

## Day Two, August 8

Today we decided on Steeple Peak. The description enticed
us with this interesting chimney. We hiked easily enough
up to the start. I just forgot to drop my poles at the
lake, so I left them where we turned left up a ramp to
gain the ridge just north of the route proper.

We often discussed a mix of politics and economics.
Robert has a strong background in economics, and knows the principles
by which prosperity occurs perfectly. My thought about that
is something like "let him cook," that is, ideally, those
principals could and should be followed by governments and
everyone wins. My fear is that schools have done their job
so poorly in educating the young, and our media have never
told us about the horror of communism, while they fill the
airways with the "perfidy" of Donald Trump making a ballroom
in the whitehouse or whatever the outrage of the day is.
So we can't convince the young to vote for the old Republican
recipie of lower taxes and balanced budgets.

Maybe the ones who were raised in something of the old way
will vote for patriotism, pride, love of country. And maybe
then we can have lower taxes and balanced budgets!

From the first belay, Robert started up a neat twin
crack system, ultimately preferring the left side. It had a tough
vertical section at the start, then eased off. He continued to
the ridge crest and built a belay on a ledge. I followed up,
and the way continued with a traverse on the right side of
the crest, kind of a leaning crack. There was one tough
move in here where I couldn't decide whether to go right
or up. Finally I went up. That was solid 5.8, I think.

In fact, I found the climbing rather intimidating! The lack of
rock climbing since early June had affected me, so I just
let Robert lead all pitches for the rest of the trip. I'd
be in "follow mode," and that was fine. I was only a bit
disappointed to miss doing some leads I know would be
deeply memorable. The climbing was memorable, anyway though,
so that was good.

Now we scrambled along the ridge and set a belay right below
the cave entrance. Robert went around the corner, touched the cave,
then did some neat moves traversing up a ramp on the outside
to gain a higher floor in the cavern. Then he went straight up
with chimney technique: back on one wall, feet on the other, and
built a belay on a pile of rocks. I came up, enjoying the pitch
very much. I attached the backpack to a sling on my harness so it
hung a couple feet below me for the chimney.

Now we moved across a mezzanine level to stand right below a
boulder overhanging the obvious exit from the chimney above us.
This looked intimidating! But it went quite nicely. Getting some
gear in a crack, Robert began the chimney technique again, then wedged
himself into a wide crack, rewarded by a difficult-to-place piece
of gear, then he could traverse right and up to reach the boulder
and build a belay on a nice ledge. So in the end, the pitch protected
well. There were some funny situations, like leaning on one wall with
feet delicately tip-toeing on a toe ledge on the other wall.

From here, with the black hole of the cave and chimney behind us, we
could have taken an easier route around a corner, but Robert chose the
spectacular 5.9 corner crack exit. I could see him for the first 30
feet of this lead, then track his progress by rope movement. Following,
this was probably the best pitch of the trip for the position right
below the summit, and the great clean and protectable climbing.
Great job, Robert!

We hung out on top for a while, then scrambled down a bit and started
a mix of rappelling and scrambling down the ridge towards Lost Temple
Spire. Eventually, we stood on ledges below the col between that
peak and Steeple, then pieced our way down.

Occasional tracks and cairns marked the way. We lost it eventually, then
contemplated a move back to the right (north), but it gave me the
heebie-jeebies and I convinced Robert to come back and continue questing
left (south). We eventually split up on loose, easy terrain, though
marked with occasional big slabby cliffs. I went directly down to the
lake, while Robert traversed over right again to more easily pick up
the backpack we'd left where we started up the ramps for the North
Ridge.

## Day Three, August 9

Today we gamely hiked up for the Minor Dihedral, but saw another party
starting out. Realizing it would take some time if we followed them,
we marched back to camp and settled on a non-technical scramble
objective: the ridge over Deep Lake Dome to East Temple.

{% image /cmaimages/2026/28_IMG_0196.jpg %}
*Two climbers on the first technical pitch of the Minor
Dihedral (5.9).*

This was great fun. I really enjoy the route-finding process...neither
too cautious nor too aggressive, we had some use of hands to get on
top of Deep Lake Dome, then a reasonable descent, then a long
climb on or near the ridge crest to the high plateau.

On top of East Temple Peak, I missed the obvious and stark white
"Diving Board," and went down to get a look at the notch separating that
peak from Lost Temple Spire. Invigorating! Robert bade me come back up
to play on the Diving Board. Wow...extreme exposure crawling up to that
edge and looking down! We took some pictures, then headed down to
the pass. I told Robert about Corey and I passing through this point
on our CDT hike 3 years before. Later, we met a couple going up to the
pass and making a loop down the south side and back to the west
to reach (presumably) the Big Sandy trailhead.

## Day Four, August 10

{% image /cmaimages/2026/16_20260809_144343.jpg %}
*The Minor Dihedral route*

**Pitch One:** Robert just walked across the ledge to set a belay for the
pitch. I couldn’t cross it!

I felt the slabs beneath pulling at me. I needed the
psychological aid of the rope.

This was upsetting. I suddenly felt useless and in the wrong
place in more ways than one.

Robert walked back and gave me a belay to cross, and I did so
easily. How ridiculous I am, I thought.

I realized what I’d been doing in the last days. I’d decided
the climbing was too hard for me, but I knew I could carry on
as long as the essential wound did not have to be exposed. I
could follow pitches, but if I was too exposed, then the wound
became too large, it in fact defined me.

Robert said something useful about that, as we walked out of
our camp when the climb was over. He said that we make an error
by drawing a line through our lives, an ever-rising line, and
feel terrible when we failed to live up to it. The line was
fiction, an abstraction. But we accept the rule of the line
over us, and die continuously when we haven’t met it. He said
that a better way to see it is that you are where you are in
each moment, and in some moments you are the master and in
others you are a passenger. Moments of mastery are born in the
times you are a passenger. You must be patient with the human
material you have built. It is imperfect. Have the patience to
improve the material, and to accept help. He called that the
brotherhood of the rope.

He is right. I hope to learn it. Sometimes the material of what
I am covers things I may have once known but forgotten. We saw
a tree with a wound on the way out.  The tree grew a bulb over
the wound, so it could live on. I have grown a bulb over a
wound, and it took something from me. Or so I came to believe.

The way through this is to throw away all definitions. All
justifications. To really enter the present moment. If I can
remember that, then there is hope.

**Pitch Two:** Robert led up the right-facing corner, very well done! It was a beautiful
pitch. Jamming and stemming. At the end, you have to climb 10-15 feet of slab.
That would have been too much for me on lead, on this day or perhaps any day!
But a ledge is reached, where small cams under an overlap provide a belay.

**Pitch Three:** Easily crossing a low angle slab, then up an easy ramp that steepens again into
another right-facing corner. This one shorter, but with a hard move near the
end where the rock bulges out with feet on slabs. A great ledge belay with a
slung block. As I came up, Robert was looking at the next pitch. We needed to
traverse a slabby face into a feature called the “scoop.” I’d read about it and
was worried that I’d find the traverse hard, even though it should only be 5.6
— apparently you had to cross at the right place.

**Pitch Four:** Right at the belay it was possible to cross the slab…there was a good
chalked-up handhold. But the feet were rather blank, and you’d be tempted to
lunge for the edge of the scoop. Once in the scoop you can place a cam in an
overlap. Robert made an even better choice, however. By climbing down about 10
feet, the slab was crossable without real handholds, but a slight lessening in
angle for the feet. He did this, then got some gear at that level in the scoop.
He then climbed up to the level of the belay and placed a cam in the overlap,
such that I could cross high without facing a big pendulum swing. The rest of
the pitch was simple — simply climb out of the scoop on the right side up a
left-facing corner to a ledge. Robert chose the highest ledge where he could
still watch me cross.

I did this, though I was glad he chose the more conservative crossing on the
lead. I think we both enjoyed the safety engineering involved here. The scoop
had been defanged! That was a real load off, and the way up to the dihedral was
now clear.


**Pitch Five:** Another pitch with a nice 5.8 hand crack got to a big ledge at the
bottom of the dihedral. Glorious!

**Pitch Six:** As Robert climbed, we saw rain to the west, currently behind Temple Peak, but
coming towards us. We weren’t that worried because the clouds tended to
dissolve right over us. However, as he tackled the 5.9 crux of the pitch, a few
raindrops landed around us. The right wall of the dihedral became a smooth
diorite wall at that point, however, the protection was good in hand and finger
cracks. Also, the left wall became more featured, offering good holds for the
left hand. Robert set a belay on a mid-point ledge. This was a great pitch!
Really fun climbing. The sun reappeared, though the gray, cloudy intervals
seemed to be coming more rapidly.

**Pitch Seven:** The second half of the dihedral was mostly easier, however the final 20 meters
were “workman-like” as Robert said. Indeed, the crux here wasn’t as sharp as
the pitch before, but the crack had widened so the jams were a bit less secure.
Finally, Robert got his whole body inside the crack, and dispatched the pitch
with a few more awkward moves. Another great ledge, and the dihedral was
dispatched!

**Pitch Eight:** Then a 50 meter pitch with twin cracks, which felt like a continuation of the
dihedral, and offered stellar climbing.

**Pitch Nine:** We joked about unroping here, as other parties had taken the guidebook
description of “4th class to the summit” too literally. An intimidating roof
lay about 15 meters above us. In fact, this was the most strenuous part of the
climb! Robert nearly used his knee on the first overhang, but then deftly got
his foot high and mantled above. On the second overhang he wasn’t so lucky!
Happily he had a good piece of gear at head level to make the hard move, but it
was certainly very awkward. Robert ran the rope out above on mostly easier
terrain. The rope came tight and I climbed. I knew he’d be looking for a belay.
Indeed, the rope stopped moving as I was about to climb the first overhang. I
was able to place a cam and wait the few minutes for those reassuring rope
movements that indicate a good belay. “Nice!” I said, and entered the
overhangs. The second one was quite tough. In fact, I started up, but couldn’t
find a place for my right foot, and I had all my weight on a left hand jam. I
think I even grabbed my wrist with the right hand. I just couldn’t do it, and
lowered myself back down. For the second try, I saw a left foothold far out
left at the level of my ears. I punched my foot up there, got the jam again,
and simulated a plank of wood by levering myself off the far-out foot and
smearing my right hand on a minor bump. Whew! I was wheezing the rest of the
pitch with the exertion! Maybe I didn’t do it the easiest way, but one thing
for sure (we joked): it was not yet time to unrope!

**Pitch Ten:** Happily, easy ground lay above. I went up into it, placing two pieces until
coming to a little alcove on a tower we’d have to climb down the opposite side
of. The wind here was screaming, and I’d had to climb down 10 feet to retrieve
the rope from a constriction. Robert arrived, and we climbed down to easy
ground where we put on regular shoes and ate some food. We scrambled to the
vague summit, then traversed over then down to the Grassy Goat Trail, helpfully
on a way marked by cairns.

We were keen to get down because the sky was cloudy again. Although we’d
descended this way before, we didn’t want to be in a storm up here!

We got down without event, making one rappel where Robert generously left a
locking carabiner around the slings. A nice Australian couple was coming up and
we chatted a while. Soon we were back at camp, enjoying a last dram of whiskey
and deciding to hike out. But first, the rain came. Worrying a bit about the
Austrailians (hoping they were hunkered down or had decided not to go up the
ramp), we sat a while with our packs inside the Megamid tarp while the storm
passed.

We hiked quickly out, dreaming of a party of food and beer at the
truck. I had the song "[Brand New Key](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RCTMTflcuug)" stuck in my head. It was interesting to
read what Melanie said about it later:


> "Brand New Key", I wrote in about fifteen minutes one night. I thought it was
> cute; a kind of old thirties tune. I guess a key and a lock have always
> been Freudian symbols, and pretty obvious ones at that. There was no deep
> serious expression behind the song, but people read things into it. They made
> up incredible stories as to what the lyrics said and what the song meant. In
> some places, it was even banned from the radio. My idea about songs is that
> once you write them, you have very little say in their life afterward. It's a
> lot like having a baby. You conceive a song, deliver it, and then give it as
> good a start as you can. After that, it's on its own. People will take it any
> way they want to take it.

We drove down to our car camp of the first night and had a great time listening to music, cooking some great food and enjoying some excellent smoked white cheddar and a very spicy peppered sausage. Awesome!
